There is something particular about a termination email at 6am. Not a conversation. Not a meeting. A message — timed to arrive before the workday, before anyone could intervene, before the human on the other end had fully woken up. Thirty thousand of them, sent by Oracle on the morning of March 31, 2026. All saying the same thing: "today is your last working day."

We talk about disruption as if it happens dramatically — a sudden rupture, a visible before and after. But this is how it actually arrives. Quietly. In your inbox. Before your first cup of chai.

What makes this moment worth sitting with isn't the number — though 60,000 tech job cuts in a single quarter is staggering. It's what was happening simultaneously on the other side of the same ledger. The same week Oracle's emails landed, India was committing to $180 billion in data center infrastructure. Google was breaking ground on its largest AI hub outside the United States — in Vizag. Reliance had just announced a $110 billion AI infrastructure bet spanning seven years. Airtel raised a billion dollars for its data center arm in one round.

The jobs that built India's middle class are contracting. The infrastructure that will define the next twenty years is expanding. Both are happening at once — and they are not unrelated.

This is not a story about doom, and it is not a story about opportunity. It is a story about a genuine shift in where value lives — what kind of work gets rewarded, what kind of country India is becoming, and what the people caught in the middle of this transition actually do. That is what the next four sections are about.

Part 01

The Work That
Looked Like Tasks
Is Already Gone

The layoff wave of 2026 has a particular shape. Understanding that shape matters more than mourning the numbers.

Oracle posted a 95% jump in net income last quarter — $6.13 billion. The same quarter it cut 30,000 people. This is the detail that deserves more attention than it gets, because it contains the whole story. This wasn't a company in crisis shedding weight to survive. It was a profitable company shedding weight to accelerate. The layoffs weren't a symptom of failure. They were a feature of a strategy.

The companies restructuring right now — Oracle, Accenture, Amazon, Salesforce, TCS, Meta, Citigroup — are not doing it despite their AI investments. They're doing it because of them. Every dollar freed from headcount is a dollar that goes into compute, infrastructure, and AI capability. The transition isn't coming. It came.

What's being cut is specific: roles where the primary output is the reliable execution of a defined process. Analytics reports produced on schedule. Tickets resolved to a template. Documents drafted from a brief. Support queues cleared. These are not low-skill jobs. Many are held by experienced, well-paid professionals. But they share a structural characteristic — the work inside them can be described precisely enough for a system to learn it. And once work can be described that precisely, it can be automated.

What remains — and what is, in fact, growing in demand — is work that cannot be fully specified in advance. Work that requires reading a room, holding a relationship, making a judgment call with incomplete information, and being accountable for the outcome. Governance. Transition management. Stakeholder navigation. Strategic advisory. These things require a human not because machines can't process the information, but because the human is part of what makes the output legitimate. A client needs to trust the person on the other side. A board needs to hold someone accountable. An organisation in change needs someone who can be in the room.

"The labour substitution narrative has shifted from hypothetical to operational." — Goldman Sachs, March 2026

The people most exposed are those whose careers have been built on doing the defined work very well, without building visibility around the judgment that sits behind it. The people most protected are those who have made their thinking — not just their output — visible and legible to the people who make decisions. The gap between those two groups is widening faster than most people realise.

The money those cuts freed didn't disappear. It went somewhere specific. ↓
Part 02

India Is Being
Rebuilt From
the Foundation Up

The infrastructure story unfolding in India right now is not incremental. It is foundational — and most people are underestimating its scale.

India generates approximately 20% of the world's data but hosts only 5.5% of global data center capacity. That is an extraordinary gap — between what this country produces and what it has built to process, store, and serve it. Closing that gap is now one of the largest capital allocation stories in the world.

What is significant about physical infrastructure — in a way that software jobs or service contracts are not — is its permanence. A gigawatt data center in Andhra Pradesh does not relocate to a lower-cost country when margins shift. It stays. It requires power, security, connectivity, skilled operations staff, and an entire ecosystem of supporting services for the twenty to thirty years of its operational life. India is, for the first time, accumulating assets in the global technology economy rather than simply providing services to it.

The policy signal supporting this is unusually clear. The Union Budget 2026 extended a tax holiday to 2047 for foreign cloud companies using Indian data center infrastructure. Twenty-one years of policy certainty is rare anywhere in the world. It is a direct acknowledgment that this administration understands what is at stake — and is willing to structure India's fiscal environment around attracting it.

For cities like Vizag, Bhopal, Ahmedabad, and Kolkata, this matters in ways that go beyond headlines. The data center economy creates demand for power infrastructure, renewable energy, construction, real estate, logistics, and decades of operational employment at every level. The technology is the anchor — but the economic activity it generates extends well beyond the server room. The question India must now answer is whether it will build AI capability alongside AI infrastructure, or simply become the world's most sophisticated landlord. Hosting the compute is the beginning of the story, not the end of it.

"The data centre story in India is no longer about potential. It's about execution at scale." — CBRE India, 2026

Part 03

How the System
Actually Works —
and Why It's Working

There is no conspiracy here. Just a set of incentives operating exactly as designed. Which is, in some ways, more unsettling.

Systems don't need intention to produce outcomes. They just need consistent incentives. And the incentives operating across this entire story — corporate, national, individual — are remarkably coherent once you see them together.

The cycle — six steps, no surprises
1
Capital bets on AI at scale. Oracle borrowed $58B in two months for infrastructure. The bet is existential — miss the AI transition and become irrelevant. The debt load is uncomfortable, but the alternative is worse.
2
Human costs are liquidated to fund the bet. 30,000 jobs freed $8–10B. The stock rose 6% the morning those emails arrived. Investors weren't celebrating cruelty — they were rewarding what they read as discipline and clarity of direction.
3
The narrative becomes "innovation." Not restructuring. Not cost pressure. Transformation. Language shapes perception — and in this case, it also shapes regulation, public acceptance, and the pace at which others follow.
4
Nations compete for the infrastructure. India offers 21 years of tax certainty. The revenue foregone is real. The physical assets gained are permanent. Both things are true simultaneously.
5
The jobs created are fewer than the jobs replaced. A 1GW data center runs on hundreds of people. The IT services workforce it helps displace numbered in thousands. The gap is real, and it accumulates quietly over time.
6
Everyone needs the cycle to continue. Companies need growth signals. Nations need investment. Workers need employment. And so the negotiation restarts, each time with the leverage sitting slightly more firmly with whoever controls the compute.

The discomfort with this picture isn't that it's wrong. It's that it's working exactly as designed — and the design doesn't have a provision for the people who built the previous system. The engineer with fifteen years of enterprise software experience. The BPO manager who built a team of forty. The analyst whose entire career was constructed around a set of processes now being automated. These people did everything right. The system just changed what "right" meant.

Knowing how the mechanism works does not mean you can stop it. But it does mean you can make better decisions inside it — about what skills are worth building, what proximity is worth pursuing, what income diversification is worth the effort, and how to position yourself in relation to the transition rather than simply in the path of it.

"On one hand, Oracle stock jumps 6%. On the other, real people just lost their jobs the very same day." — Blind, March 2026
Which brings us to the only question that actually matters from here. ↓
Part 04

What You
Actually Do
From Here

Not a motivational list. A set of honest observations about what separates people who navigate this well from those who don't.

The most common mistake being made right now isn't ignorance — it's misdiagnosis. People who feel the disruption tend to respond in one of two ways: paralysis (waiting to see how it unfolds) or overreaction (pivoting entirely into something unrecognisable). The people who navigate this best do neither. They make one or two precise moves — building a specific capability, making a specific relationship visible, creating one alternative income stream — and they do it before the pressure forces them to.

The roles that are genuinely growing — AI governance, transition leadership, cloud compliance, data strategy advisory — are not pure technology roles. They require someone who understands the organisation, can hold relationships under pressure, and can be accountable for outcomes in ambiguous situations. The technology is the context. The human capability is the scarce resource. If your career has been built around IT services, consulting, BPO, or programme management — that foundation is not obsolete. It needs a new layer on top of it.

✓ What moves the needle

  • Build AI fluency in the domain you already own — governance, oversight, prompt design. Not a career change. An upgrade.
  • Make your thinking visible — through writing, through speaking, through positions taken. Output can be automated. Perspective cannot.
  • Diversify income now, while you have the stability to do it thoughtfully — not as a panic response later.
  • Understand the India infrastructure story and find your connection to it — advisory, operations, policy, or investment. The opportunity window is early.
  • Build relationships laterally into AI-adjacent functions inside your current organisation. Visibility to decision-makers is not optional anymore.
  • Treat your career like a portfolio — not one asset, but several. Some legacy, some transitional, some speculative.

✗ What wastes the window

  • Waiting for your employer to define your next skill set. By the time that conversation happens, the decision has usually already been made.
  • Assuming that doing your current job well is sufficient protection. Oracle's 40-year veteran received the same email as everyone else.
  • Competing with AI systems on volume, speed, or consistency. That race is lost before it starts.
  • Treating the India infrastructure story as someone else's opportunity — it isn't geographically or professionally distant from most people reading this.
  • Conflating activity with positioning. Being productive inside a shrinking function is not the same as being strategically placed.

The honest truth about disruption at this scale is that it will not be navigated by any single clever move. It will be navigated by a series of small, deliberate decisions made over the next eighteen to thirty-six months — about what to learn, where to be visible, how to earn, and who to be in proximity to. The people who come through this well won't have done something extraordinary. They will have started earlier than felt necessary.

The 6am email was sent without malice. That is the part that deserves the most reflection. Nobody at Oracle wanted to ruin thirty thousand mornings. The system simply ran its logic to its conclusion — and the conclusion was an email. The question is not whether the system will keep running. It will. The question is whether you are building something it cannot simply email away.
