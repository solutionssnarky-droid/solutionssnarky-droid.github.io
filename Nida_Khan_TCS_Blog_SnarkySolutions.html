Editorial

The TCS Nashik Case: When the Gatekeeper Becomes the Problem

Nida Khan was responsible for POSH compliance. Now she's under investigation. A data-backed, unbiased take on workplace trust, real incidents, and what leadership actually looks like.

Snarky Solutions · April 2026 · 14 min read

Somewhere right now, a corporate WhatsApp group is buzzing. A LinkedIn post is being dissected. And leadership teams across India are quietly asking, "Could this happen to us?"

Because a story like the one unfolding at TCS's Nashik BPO unit doesn't just trend. It triggers. Not because people suddenly care about one HR manager. But because they see themselves — or someone they know — in the headline.

This piece isn't about taking sides. It's about asking the questions that every organisation in India needs to sit with — honestly, uncomfortably, and without the safety net of a press release.

Part I

What Happened at TCS Nashik

In late March 2026, a young woman working at a TCS-linked BPO facility in Nashik walked into the Deolali Camp police station and filed a complaint. She alleged that between 2022 and 2026 — four years — she had been subjected to sexual harassment, inappropriate remarks, and coercion by colleagues in her office.

What followed was an avalanche.

By early April, eight more employees — seven women and one man — had come forward. Nine FIRs were registered across two police stations. The allegations ranged from sexual harassment and inappropriate physical contact to sustained mental pressure and offensive remarks about religious beliefs. Most complainants were women between the ages of 18 and 25.

A Special Investigation Team (SIT) was constituted by the Nashik Police. Seven people were arrested — six men and one woman — including team leaders and an operations manager. Among those named: Nida Khan, a Pune-based HR manager at the facility. The person whose job it was to handle employee grievances and ensure POSH compliance was now being investigated as a central figure in how those very complaints were allegedly ignored.

The SIT is currently examining 78 emails, WhatsApp chats, call records, and over 40 CCTV recordings to determine whether complaints were deliberately suppressed.

⚠ The detail that should stop every leader

The case was partly uncovered through a covert police operation. Women officers were deployed inside the facility posing as housekeeping staff for nearly two weeks, observing employee interactions and reporting findings to senior investigators. Law enforcement had to go undercover inside a corporate office to uncover what the company's own systems should have caught years earlier.

TCS responded by affirming its "long-standing, zero-tolerance policy towards harassment and coercion of any form." The employees under investigation were suspended. N. Chandrasekaran, Chairman of Tata Sons, ordered an internal probe. The company's CHRO, Sudeep Kunnumal, issued a company-wide communication to all TCS employees across India, directing them to use the Ethics Concern portal on Ultimatix or write to ic.posh@tcs.com.

Meanwhile, NITES (the Nashik IT employees' union) called for a comprehensive audit of POSH compliance across IT and ITES companies in Maharashtra — pointing out that they had flagged the absence of effective grievance systems in the sector to the government as far back as 2022.

The investigation is ongoing. All accused are entitled to the presumption of innocence until proven otherwise.

But the questions this case raises — about trust, about systems, about the distance between policy and practice — don't belong to TCS alone. They belong to every organisation in India.

Part II

This Isn't New. And That's the Problem.

The TCS Nashik case feels shocking. But if you've been paying attention, it's part of a pattern that's been escalating for years.

August 2024

R.G. Kar Medical College, Kolkata

A 31-year-old trainee doctor was raped and murdered inside a seminar hall after working a 36-hour shift. The case exposed catastrophic failures in institutional safety — the ICC existed on paper, but the workplace had no functional rest areas, no CCTV in vulnerable zones, no security protocols for night shifts. Over a million doctors went on strike nationally. The Supreme Court took suo motu cognisance, constituted a National Task Force on doctor safety, and asked a pointed question: "If women are not safe at work, we are denying them equality."

January 2025

HCL Technologies — Madras High Court Ruling

A supervisor was found guilty of sexually harassing three women employees — insisting on handshakes in close physical proximity, touching a colleague's shoulder and suggesting she remove her clothes, and asking another about her menstrual cycle. The Madras High Court established a landmark principle: it's the impact on the woman that matters, not the intent of the accused. The IC's findings were upheld, and the Labour Court's intervention was overturned. The ruling signalled to Corporate India that "I didn't mean it that way" is no longer a defence.

April 2026

Infosys BPM, Pune

Days after the TCS Nashik story broke, social media posts alleged harassment of female employees at an Infosys BPM facility in Pune. The posts were later deleted, but not before Maharashtra Minister Nitesh Rane confirmed the government had taken note. Infosys issued a statement reiterating its zero-tolerance policy and activating internal processes. The incident underscored how quickly contagion spreads — one case emboldens others to speak, and suddenly an entire sector is under scrutiny.

August 2025

Supreme Court Directive — Aureliano Fernandes v. State of Goa

The Supreme Court directed all states and union territories to conduct district-wise surveys to verify whether organisations had even constituted Internal Complaints Committees as required under the POSH Act. Twelve years after the law was passed, the highest court in the country had to check if basic compliance existed. The Court also ordered that data be uploaded to the SHe-Box platform for centralised monitoring.

These aren't isolated incidents. They're data points in a pattern. Different industries, different cities, different companies — but the same underlying failure: systems that exist on paper but don't function in practice.

A Business Standard analysis of 300 NSE-listed companies found that over 10,300 sexual harassment cases were registered under POSH between FY14 and FY25. Roughly 14% of all cases remain unresolved. The IT sector alone accounted for 33.2% of all complaints in FY25 — followed by financial services at 33.1%.

In FY25, Wipro reported 195 POSH complaints. TCS reported 125. ICICI Bank reported 117.

And here's the uncomfortable kicker: POSH complaints account for a mere 0.1% of the total female workforce across NSE 300 companies. That's not a sign that things are fine. That's a sign that the vast majority of harassment still goes unreported.

A 2025 policy brief backed by the UK Foreign Office surveyed women across India's corporate, finance, and consulting sectors. Nearly 70% reported harassment. Sixty percent reported sexist hostility. Forty-four percent reported unwanted sexual attention. Thirteen percent reported unwelcome touching. Ten percent reported indecent exposure.

These aren't numbers from a developing-world stereotype. These are numbers from the offices of some of India's most admired companies.

Part IV

The Real Problem Isn't Process. It's Trust.

Every large company in India will tell you: "We have a process."

Grievance portals. Ethics hotlines. HR business partners. Skip-level meetings. POSH committees with external members and quarterly awareness sessions.

On paper, it's a masterpiece.

In reality, it often depends on who you are, who you report to, and how inconvenient your complaint is.

A process people don't trust is just documentation. Not a system.

In the TCS Nashik case, the person responsible for POSH compliance was allegedly part of the problem. The HR manager — the gatekeeper — was among those named.

This is the structural flaw the POSH Act doesn't adequately address: What happens when the gatekeeper is the problem?

The Act assumes good faith at the institutional level. It assumes the ICC will be impartial. It assumes HR will act. It assumes the culture supports reporting. When any of those assumptions break down — and in Nashik, they appear to have all broken down simultaneously — the architecture collapses.

And employees are left with exactly two options: stay silent, or go to the police.

When someone goes public or files an FIR, leadership often reacts with: "Why didn't they escalate internally?"

But the more honest question is: Why did they feel the need to go outside at all?

Because escalation isn't just about availability. It's about belief. Do employees believe they'll be heard? Do they believe action will follow? Do they believe it won't backfire?

If the answer is even slightly "no," your process is already broken — no matter how many portals you've built or how beautifully your policy is drafted.

Part V

The Silent Majority No One Talks About

For every case that makes headlines, there are hundreds of people who stay quiet. Adjust. Disengage. Or eventually leave.

No LinkedIn post. No FIR. No viral thread. Just silent exits and declining morale.

Research shows that approximately 33% of victims choose to leave their organisation rather than report. Another 33% don't know what their options are. And 17% simply stay and endure.

That's not resilience. That's resignation.

And it's actually more dangerous than the viral cases. Because silence doesn't mean things are fine. Silence means people have decided your system isn't worth engaging with.

Think about the colleague who stopped speaking up in meetings. The one who used to stay late but now leaves at 5:30 sharp. The one who's been "exploring opportunities" on LinkedIn for months. The one who smiles and says "everything's fine" but hasn't brought a single idea to the table in six months.

That's what unaddressed workplace toxicity looks like in practice. Not a dramatic exit. Not a viral post. Just slow, steady erosion — of talent, of trust, of the very culture that companies spend millions trying to build.

The most expensive thing a company can own is a grievance system that everyone knows exists — and no one trusts enough to use.

Part VI

What Real Leadership Looks Like

This isn't a TCS problem. It isn't an IT sector problem. It's a Corporate India problem.

And the organisations that get ahead of it won't be the ones with the best crisis communications or the most expensive legal teams. They'll be the ones who build trust before it's tested.

Here's what that actually takes:

Audit trust, not just process

Your POSH compliance may be 100% on paper. But do employees actually believe the system will protect them? Anonymous pulse surveys. External assessments. Conversations that go beyond "How are things going?" You need to know what people are not telling you — because that's where the risk lives.

Separate the gatekeeper from the gate

If your HR team is both the POSH compliance owner and the party being complained about, you've created a structural conflict of interest. Build in external oversight. Give employees reporting channels that don't route through their direct chain of command. The Nashik case is a textbook example of what happens when this separation doesn't exist.

Reward early escalation

Employees who raise red flags early should be treated as assets, not inconveniences. The cost of one early complaint, properly handled, is infinitely lower than the cost of nine FIRs and a national investigation. Organisations that punish dissent — even subtly, even through inaction — are training their people to stay silent.

Fix response time, not just resolution

An employee who files a complaint and hears nothing for three months has already learned that reporting is pointless. Speed of acknowledgement matters. Communicate timelines. Close the loop. Every single time. Even if you can't share the outcome, acknowledge the complaint and follow up.

Train managers — not just HR

Most harassment goes unreported not because employees don't know the policy, but because their immediate manager is either the problem or isn't equipped to handle it. In a world where 70% of women experience some form of workplace harassment, manager training on recognising, responding to, and escalating issues isn't a nice-to-have — it's a frontline necessity.

Make the IC genuinely independent

The POSH Act requires an external member on the ICC. That shouldn't be a formality. Invest in IC members who are trained, empowered, and not beholden to the leadership they're supposed to hold accountable. The Madras High Court's 2025 HCL ruling made clear: a properly constituted IC's findings carry legal weight. Treat it accordingly.

Part VII

The Bigger Conversation

The easy narrative here is outrage. The easier one is defence.

The harder — and more important — conversation is this:

India's POSH Act was a landmark. The Vishaka Guidelines of 1997 were visionary. The legislative intent was sound. But legislation alone has never changed a culture. Implementation has. Accountability has. Leadership has.

We have a law that's been in effect for over twelve years. The Supreme Court is still directing states to check whether companies have even formed Internal Committees. Complaints have grown nearly tenfold — but 14% remain unresolved. And the overwhelming majority of harassment still goes unreported.

This isn't a moment for finger-pointing. It's a moment for mirror-holding.

Every leader reading this headline has a choice: treat this as someone else's problem, or use it as a prompt to ask the uncomfortable questions about your own organisation.

Not "Do we have a policy?" — you almost certainly do.

But: "Do our people trust it?"

• • •

The Uncomfortable Bottom Line

If employees feel heard internally, they don't go to the police externally. If they do, it's not a PR problem. It's a leadership problem.

TCS will navigate this — they have the weight of the Tata Group, a swift institutional response, and the resources to course-correct. The real test isn't whether TCS survives this news cycle.

It's whether the next employee, at the next company, will trust the internal system enough to report — or whether she'll also have to wait four years and hope someone notices.

Organisations don't lose control when employees speak out.
They lose control when employees stop believing they'll be heard.

The strongest workplaces aren't the ones where nothing goes wrong.
They're the ones where something can go wrong — and people still believe the system will make it right.

Build that kind of workplace. That's the only policy that works.
