15 Minutes vs 3 Days. Who’s Actually Busy?

An unanswered message is somebody else’s unfinished work.

15 minthe director with 9 meetings
3 days“been super busy”

We’ve all experienced this.

You send someone a message. They’re online. They attend meetings, share updates and stay active everywhere except your inbox.

Three days later: “Hey! Sorry, been super busy.”

Meanwhile, someone running an entire department, juggling deadlines, travelling between meetings and making real decisions replies in 15 minutes.

Plot twist: the problem isn’t how busy they are. It’s how they handle attention.

1. Busy people understand the cost of waiting

People in demanding environments develop an instinct for prioritisation. They know an unanswered message can become:

  • A delayed decision
  • A blocked dependency
  • An unnecessary follow-up
  • A frustrated stakeholder
  • Another problem waiting to become urgent

They don’t reply instantly to everything. They know which message needs a response, which needs a decision and which can wait. A short acknowledgement prevents hours of chasing.

“Received. I’ll review this by tomorrow.”

Five seconds. Zero chasing.

Snarky aside: “Seen” without a reply is not a status. It’s a cliffhanger.

2. Responsive is not the same as available

Being responsive doesn’t mean being permanently reachable. You can have excellent communication habits and still hold boundaries. There are three different behaviours:

Instant availabilityReplying to everything immediately, usually at the expense of focused work.
Intentional responsivenessAcknowledging, prioritising and setting realistic expectations. The sweet spot.
Communication avoidanceLeaving messages unanswered because replying is inconvenient, uncomfortable or needs a decision.

The most effective professionals don’t have more time. They create clarity around the time they have.

3. Why people postpone simple replies

  • Decision avoidance: the message needs a difficult answer, so it waits.
  • Perfectionism: they want a complete answer, not a quick acknowledgement. The perfect reply becomes a very late reply.
  • Cognitive overload: too many demands make even small decisions exhausting.
  • Task-switching: constant context changes make consistent communication harder.
  • Low perceived priority: the sender or the request just isn’t getting attention.

None of it is always deliberate. The impact still lands on the other person.

Snarky aside: “I’ll reply when I have a perfect answer” is how a 2-line reply becomes a 3-day saga.

4. What effective communicators do differently

The Eisenhower Matrix. Separate urgent from important. Not every message needs an instant reply, but important dependencies shouldn’t vanish into an inbox.

Getting Things Done (David Allen). Capture the request, clarify the action, decide the next step. Don’t let every message live in your head as an unfinished obligation.

The Two-Minute Rule. If a reply takes under two minutes, do it rather than letting it pile up.

Time blocking. Set windows for email, messages and follow-ups. Fewer interruptions, and communication doesn’t become an afterthought.

The shared principle: don’t depend on remembering to respond. Build a system that makes responding easy.

5. The hidden professional cost

In professional life, communication is part of execution. You can deliver excellent work and still have a reliability problem if people repeatedly chase you for basic updates. Over time, they may:

  • Stop involving you in important decisions
  • Find alternative stakeholders
  • Escalate routine matters
  • Build workarounds around you

Your work might be great. Your reputation takes the hit. And reputation decides who gets the bigger job.

Snarky aside: nobody promotes “brilliant, but you have to chase them.”

6. The practical toolkit: urgent vs important

One of the biggest productivity mistakes is treating everything that demands attention as something that deserves priority.

Urgency is about time. Importance is about consequence.

A message marked URGENT isn’t automatically important. A task with no deadline isn’t automatically unimportant.

The four-quadrant matrix

Q1 · Do

Urgent + ImportantClient escalations, delivery blockers, compliance deadlines, anything due today.

Q2 · Schedule

Important + Not urgentDeveloping your team, improving processes, learning, planning your next career move. Often neglected because nothing is screaming for it.

Q3 · Delegate

Urgent + Not importantRoutine status updates, scheduling requests, standard admin.

Q4 · Eliminate

Not urgent + Not importantMeetings without objectives, repeat discussions with no decisions, low-value notifications.

You don’t need to attend every meeting to prove you’re busy. A calendar full of Q4 is not a flex.

The 60-second decision test

Before postponing anything, ask:

  1. Will delaying this create a problem for someone else?
  2. Is there a deadline or dependency involved?
  3. Does this need my decision, or can someone else handle it?
  4. Is this contributing to a meaningful outcome?
  5. Can I acknowledge it now and commit to a realistic response time?

Your inbox should not dictate your priorities. You should.

7. Five habits that actually help

1. Acknowledge quicklyEven if you can’t solve it yet, confirm you’ve received it.

2. Give a realistic timelineReplace silence with an expected response time.

3. Close the loopSay when something is done, delayed or blocked.

4. Batch communicationReserve time for messages instead of checking compulsively.

5. Don’t confuse avoidance with prioritisationIf it’s important, schedule it. Don’t keep postponing it.

A template you can steal:

“Received. I’m currently occupied with another priority. I’ll review this and get back to you by [time/date].”

No drama. No essay. No ghosting.

8. Time management is relationship management

Responsiveness says something beyond the content of the message:

  • I respect your time.
  • I know your work may depend on mine.
  • I can be trusted to close a loop.
  • You don’t need to chase me for every update.

That applies to managers, colleagues, clients, friends and family. Not every message deserves a reply, and boundaries are healthy. But using busyness as the standing explanation for poor communication isn’t a productivity strategy.

It’s just poor communication with a better excuse.

The Snarky Take

Busy people aren’t faster because they have more time. They’re faster because they know that an unanswered message is sometimes another person’s unfinished work.

You don’t have to reply to everyone immediately. But if everyone has to chase you eventually, your calendar isn’t the only thing that needs better management.

Stop glorifying being busy. Start getting better at being effective.
