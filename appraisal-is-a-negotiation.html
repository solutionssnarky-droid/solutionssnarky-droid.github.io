Your appraisal isn’t a verdict. It’s a negotiation you didn’t prepare for.

Most of us walk into appraisal season like students walking into an exam we never revised for, then act shocked by the result. Your manager has data, a budget and a calibration meeting. You have “but I worked really hard this year.”

6 min read, plus 2 minutes of playing with the tools · For Indian professionals · November 2026

The number everyone is quoting

Two big surveys landed on the same figure for 2026. Aon’s salary survey, covering 1,400+ organisations across 45 industries, projects an average increase of 9.1% for India. Deloitte’s India Talent Outlook also projects 9.1%, up from 9.0% in 2025.

9.1%Projected average hike, 2026 (Aon and Deloitte)
6.9%Projected average for IT services, the lowest of the major sectors (Deloitte)
14%Employees promoted in 2025, up from 12% in 2024 (Deloitte)

Here is the part that matters. An average is a midpoint, not a promise. Aon’s own rewards leader points out that organisations are putting their money behind top talent, with much higher percentages for superior performance than for average performers.

So the real question isn’t “Will I get 9%?” It’s “What evidence do I have that I belong above the average?”

Your rating is calibrated, not just given

Deloitte found that the share of employees receiving the top rating on a five-point scale fell from 10% in 2024 to 7% in 2025, while around 16% now sit in the bottom two rating bands. Promotions still rose. Translation: your manager doesn’t decide alone. They defend you in a room, against other managers defending their people, with a fixed budget.

Your manager can only argue what they can remember and prove. That makes you responsible for handing them ammunition.

Step 1: Keep receipts (the Evidence File)

Start this today, not the night before your review. Ten minutes every Friday is enough.

Log thisExample
ImpactCut month-end reporting from 5 days to 3
ScopeNow handling 3 stakeholders where the role says 1
Money or timeSaved 12 hours a week for a team of 10
Risks avoidedFlagged a dependency early, avoided a delivery slip
RecognitionPraise from a client or senior stakeholder, with date and name
Skills addedCertification completed, tool mastered, people mentored

Numbers beat adjectives. “Reliable and hardworking” describes a personality. “Reduced rework by 30%” describes a contribution.

Step 2: Check your market value

Salary conversations collapse when you only compare yourself to yourself. Triangulate instead:

Inside your company

Know your band, your level and what the next level requires. Ask what “exceeds expectations” looks like for your role, in writing if possible.

Outside your company

Look at current openings for your role and level. You don’t need to apply. You need a number that isn’t anchored in your own employer’s budget.

Your sector

Sector matters a great deal. In Deloitte’s 2026 projections, renewables lead at 10.4% while IT services sits at the bottom of the major sectors at 6.9%. Aon’s survey puts technology consulting and IT services at roughly 6.8%. Know where your industry stands before you ask for something its budget can’t stretch to.

Step 3: Prepare the conversation

Don’t improvise. Use if/then planning, which research on implementation intentions shows helps people actually follow through on difficult actions.

Tap each scenario to reveal your line.

If they open with the rating

“Thank you. Can we walk through the criteria for the next rating level, and where you see the gap between my work and that level?”

If they say the budget is tight

“I understand. If the increment is limited, can we agree on a title change, a wider scope, or a review in six months with clear targets?”

If they say “you’re doing great, just keep going”

“Great. What specifically would I need to deliver to be promoted in the next cycle? Can we put those goals on paper?”

If you disagree with the outcome

“I’d like to understand how this was decided. Can I share a summary of my contributions for the calibration discussion or for next year’s review?”

Notice what these have in common. None of them is an ultimatum. They turn a verdict into a conversation with next steps.

Step 4: Know what else you can ask for

If the money is capped, the negotiation isn’t over. Ask for what’s still on the table:

  • A title or level change that raises your market value
  • A larger scope, a visible project or a leadership role
  • A learning or certification budget
  • Flexibility in hours or location
  • A dated, written review point instead of “we’ll see”

The loyalty tax: yes, it’s real

Staying put is a legitimate choice. But if your increments have tracked the average year after year while your scope has grown, you are paying a quiet tax for staying. Compare your current pay to what the market offers for your current responsibilities, not your original job description. If the gap is large, the conversation is about more than this year’s hike.

A reality check

Not every disappointing result is a failure of preparation. Budgets get frozen, sectors slow down and some managers will not advocate for you no matter what you bring. Preparation raises your odds. It doesn’t guarantee an outcome. If you’ve done everything right and the answer is still no, that is useful information about whether this employer is investing in your future.

Your pre-appraisal plan

  1. This weekStart the Evidence File. Write down your top five contributions with numbers.
  2. Next 2 weeksCheck your band and level. Scan five external openings for your role.
  3. Before the meetingWrite a one-page summary. Send it to your manager ahead of time.
  4. In the roomUse your if/then lines. Ask for next-level criteria and a date for the next review.
  5. After the meetingSend a short written recap of what was agreed. Calendar the follow-up.

Appraisal season doesn’t reward the hardest worker. It rewards the person whose work is easiest to defend in a room they’re not in.
