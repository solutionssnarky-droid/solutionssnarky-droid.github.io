Shine Bright for Voyager’s Light-Day

NASA is inviting the whole world to light up the night sky after sunset on 17 Nov. Here’s how to join →

Countdown to 18 Nov 2026, 10:16:07 UTC · 3:46 PM IST. Yes, right in the post-lunch slump.

A 48-hour reply time. Zero follow-ups.

On 18 November, Voyager 1 becomes the first human-made object to sit one full light-day from Earth: about 25.9 billion km. Light, the fastest thing there is, needs 24 hours to get there. A “hello” and its reply take 48.

No “seen at 10:42.” No “just circling back.” You send, you go home, you wait two days. NASA’s own Voyager project manager describes it almost exactly that way.

Not to scale. Neither is our sense of urgency.

Remember when it looked back?

In February 1990, Voyager 1 was about 6 billion km out when it turned its camera around and took the “Pale Blue Dot”: Earth, less than a single pixel. Then NASA switched the cameras off for good, to save power for the road ahead.

That was the first of many trade-offs. Thirty-six years later, the probe is four times farther out, and the whole mission is a masterclass in choosing what to give up.

Earth was less than one pixel. Every meeting you’ve ever had is in there.

The plot twist: it survives by subtraction

Voyager’s nuclear power source loses roughly 4 watts every year. So the team keeps switching things off. Of the 10 instruments it launched with, only a few still run. In September, engineers swapped three thermal devices for lower-power ones in a months-in-the-making operation they nicknamed the “Big Bang.” Without it, another instrument might have gone dark by summer 2027.

And in October, Voyager 1 went silent for about 48 hours. No signal on two scheduled check-ins. It had quietly switched to backup thrusters and stabilised itself, and then came back on 20 October. NASA still doesn’t know exactly why.

The farthest thing we’ve ever built stays alive by doing less, on purpose.

Why this actually matters

A first for humanity

No human-made object has ever been a full light-day from home. Every other spacecraft we’ve built is closer.

Irreplaceable science

Voyager 1 and 2 are the only spacecraft ever to directly measure the space outside the Sun’s bubble. No other mission can do it right now.

A clock is ticking

Power falls ~4 W a year. Without September’s power swap, another instrument could have gone dark by summer 2027. Every watt is a decision.

A shared moment

NASA’s light-day event asks everyone, anywhere, to do one simple thing at the same time. That’s rare, and it’s free.

Meanwhile, back on the grid

Us

More titles. More followers. More tabs. More “quick syncs.” Every promotion moves the finish line.

Voyager

Less power. Fewer instruments. One mission: keep the signal alive and the data honest.

We are the species that sent a message across 25.9 billion km, and we still can’t say “I’m overloaded” across a dinner table. On the Golden Record we packed greetings in 55 languages and 115 images. Then we built a world where success means collecting more of everything.

Aliens reading the record would meet a very polite species. Aliens reading our calendars would meet a very tired one.

Try the Voyager Audit (10 minutes)

  1. List your “instruments”: every commitment, meeting, group and habit.
  2. Mark the power draw: what costs you most energy for least return?
  3. Switch one off this week. Don’t apologise. Voyager didn’t.
  4. Keep the backup: one person you can go quiet around and who won’t panic.

Switch one off. Then switch one on.

Voyager can’t take more power from us. But NASA has found a way for the rest of us to send it a little light. Shine Bright for Voyager’s Light-Day is a worldwide, symbolic moment: after sunset on Tuesday 17 November, wherever you are, point a light at the sky.

How to join (2 minutes)
  1. After sunset on 17 Nov, step outside (or to a window or terrace).
  2. Grab any light: phone torch, flashlight, bike light, glow stick or solar lantern.
  3. Point it skyward. NASA’s site can flash V-G-R, Voyager’s call sign, in Morse code on your phone.
    4. ···−  −−·  ·−·
  4. Film it, post it, tag @NASA and tag me and a friend who needs to log off.

Sign-up and the phone Morse tool: go.nasa.gov/shinebright. Pair it with your Voyager Audit: power one thing down, light one thing up.

Welcome, everyone. Bring your team, your family, your WhatsApp group or your whole building. The more of us who shine, the better the story we get to tell the aliens.

Crossing one light-day changes nothing physical for Voyager. It will never know. We pick the milestone; the probe just keeps going.

Maybe the point was never to own more of the universe. It’s to stay in contact with it.

Today’s question: if you could power down one thing in your life and still keep your signal strong, what goes first?
